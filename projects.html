NASA TechRise · 2026

NDVI Mapping via Machine Learning

With NASA/Futureengineers mentorship, Team A designed an experiment to test whether machine learning could estimate the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) from a suborbital balloon flight. The payload gathered location, altitude, orientation, 11-band spectral measurements, ground imagery, and air pressure. The team planned to pair ground images with Sentinel-2 NDVI data, then compare model architectures, sensor combinations, and loss functions to explore balloon-based vegetation mapping as a lower-cost crop-health monitoring approach.

The payload flew on a high-altitude World View balloon from Paige, Arizona. Its flightbox shattered on descent, and only one SD card was recovered. Zafir Shamsi used the available data to teach team members machine learning, and the team later submitted a research paper to NeurIPS. See related work on the Research tab.

Normalized Difference Vegetation Index

NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red)

Healthy leaves strongly reflect near-infrared light, while chlorophyll absorbs red light. Comparing those reflectance values helps describe vegetation.

Payload hardware

Sensor suite

The flightbox integrated an AS7343 multispectral sensor, a TSL2591 light sensor, a BME280 environmental sensor, and an MPU6050 IMU alongside World View GPS telemetry, a photodiode, and a development board.

Data systems

Telemetry, logging & status

Every second, the Python logger writes 39 comma-separated fields—including World View flight telemetry and sensor readings—to an onboard SD card.

Sensors initialize independently; unavailable sensors are recorded as “N/A” while readings from the others continue. A NeoPixel displays flight phase—yellow for initializing, green for launching, cyan for floating, blue for terminating, and gray for unknown—while the GO LED indicates payload readiness and telemetry streaming.
